# Many hands conduct light on WebGPU

Tactilume Desktop should be an original **many-handed light instrument**, not a bigger Uzu: a custom WebGPU compute engine inside an **Electron 45** shell, in which every phone and up to eight gamepads each own a colour, a tool and a sound, music bends what the hands are doing rather than taking over, and HDR highlights at 120–240 Hz, held in check by an always-on flash limiter, are the visual signature. Electron ships one bundled Chromium engine (version 156) on Windows, macOS and Linux, so WebGPU, the Gamepad API, WebHID, system-audio capture and a Node server for the phones all live in one codebase, with the existing WebGL2 renderer kept as the fallback for Linux AMD machines, virtual machines and repeated GPU failures. No browser engine offers hardware ray tracing, but additive light barely needs it: floor pools, reflections and volumetric glow from emitters that never block each other reduce to blurs and second splats that compute shaders already do, so a native rewrite pays off only if ray tracing or DLSS-class upscaling become hard requirements. Public system requirements should come in **four tiers keyed on memory bandwidth**, from Minimum on an Iris Xe, Radeon 780M, Apple M1 or GTX 1650 at 1080p and 60 Hz with about 1–2 million particles to Ultra on an RTX 5080 or 5090 at 4K, while the default preset on every machine is **"Auto (this machine)"**, set by a half-minute first-run measurement, so that the user's own PC, and any PC with the same GPU, display and power profile, starts at its measured tier. The biggest risks are that every particle budget is modelled rather than measured, that HDR output, phone-to-screen latency and Linux audio capture are untested, and that Android 17 now blocks the Tactilume app's local-network traffic unless it uses the system's device picker. Uzu's finger-count modes made it a 2010 hit, and its users then spent a decade asking for video capture, sharing, colour control and stability, all of which a desktop successor can deliver without borrowing Uzu's names, mode ladder, presets or look.

## Uzu turned ten fingers into a light show, then stalled

Uzu is a one-person indie app by designer-engineer Jason Kent Smith under the Colordodge Labs brand, launched for the original iPad on **12 June 2010 at about US$0.99** ([AppAdvice](https://appadvice.com/app/uzu-an-interactive-light-show/376551723); [Tapscape](https://www.tapscape.com/?p=1320)). It reached #1 soon after Apple opened the iPad App Store ([MacStories](https://www.macstories.net/ipad/uzu-for-ipad-as-played-by-non-ipad-users-video/)), and Smith's portfolio calls it the first iPad app to use all ten fingers, a claim nobody has checked independently ([jasonkentsmith.com](http://jasonkentsmith.com/colordodge/)). An iPhone spin-off, Little Uzu, followed in 2010, then a universal v3.0 in 2014 ([AppFollow](https://apps.appfollow.io/ios/uzu-an-interactive-light-show/376551723)) and v3.2.1 in September 2021 ([soft112](https://uzu-a-generative-design-playground-ios.soft112.com/)). Version 4.0 rebuilt the app on Metal with a SwiftUI interface, 67 "refreshed" presets, a ten-effect post-processing bundle, preset import and export, and "Low" to "Ultra" quality settings, and the app is still on sale at **version 4.2 for US$3.99**, "Designed for iPad" and requiring iPadOS 17.6 ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/uzu-an-interactive-light-show/id376551723)). No Colordodge product exists for Windows or Linux, but Apple offers compatible iPad apps on Apple-silicon Macs unless the developer opts out, so macOS is possibly contested until someone checks the Mac App Store by hand ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/apple-silicon/running-your-ios-apps-in-macos)). Smith's newer work, Kinetiform and the fluid toy Fluidium, stays on Apple platforms ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/fluidium/id6745104628)).

The design was a vocabulary of finger chords. **The number of fingers on the glass, from zero to ten, chose one global animation mode**: Brownian drift at rest, radiating particles for one finger, attraction for two, vortices for three and four, pseudo-3D freezes, spirals and springy ribbons up to nine, and a colour-and-size gesture at ten ([Digital Mom Blog](https://www.digitalmomblog.com/baby-and-the-ipad-app-uzu/); [App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/uzu-an-interactive-light-show/id376551723)). Nothing but the art appeared on screen, an auto-play mode added virtual touches of its own, later versions reacted to the microphone, and the listing pitched an **external display with the iPad as the controller** for social gatherings ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/uzu-an-interactive-light-show/id376551723)), the direct precedent for Tactilume's handheld-drives-the-big-screen idea. The scheme needed no tutorial, worked for a toddler's palm and an expert's seven-finger chord, and tied particles tightly to fingers. Its weaknesses were built in: the mapping was invisible, so people found modes by accident, lifting one finger changed the mode, and none of it translates to a phone held in one hand or to a gamepad.

Reception was warm but shallow. Press coverage came as blurbs from Wired and Ars Technica, the listing reports Apple accolades nobody has verified, and snapshots show **4.8–4.9 stars** ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/uzu-an-interactive-light-show/id376551723)). The audiences that stayed were mostly not gamers: toddlers and families; therapists, who use it for fine motor skills, sensory needs and breathing-paced relaxation ([BridgingApps](https://bridgingapps.org/bridgingapps-reviewed-app-uzu-a-generative-design-playground/)); autistic users, who seek it out because it soothes ([The Curated Culture](https://thecuratedculture.com/im-sharing-my-favorite-sensory-apps-because-everyone-needs-one-sometimes/)); and party crowds looking for "psychedelic fireworks". A decade of user reviews complains about crashes after updates and gaps of years between releases, and asks for **video recording and export, an adjustable background colour, the return of hidden controls and "randomize all", and direct sharing** ([App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/376551723?see-all=reviews&platform=iphone)). A recent snapshot puts Uzu at #181 in Entertainment: remembered fondly, rarely discovered.

The lesson is an open slot, not a template. Across touch toys, fluid toys, music visualisers, artificial-life simulations and professional tools, **no surveyed product combines desktop-class GPU particles, several simultaneous phone and gamepad players, two-way audio and instant consumer play**, an absence the research could not prove but found consistently. Professional tools have the scale but not the play, as TouchDesigner's GPU point operators of 2025–26 show ([Derivative](https://derivative.ca/release/202532460/74120)); mobile toys have the play but not the scale; and the closest new desktop analogue, Atomic Particle Engine (Steam, April 2026), takes only mouse and music input ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/4513520/Atomic_Particle_Engine/)). Older works supply the missing pieces. Panoramical showed that gamepad or MIDI "dimensions" over curated audio-visual worlds work on desktop ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2015-09-18-panoramical-finji-polytron-indie-fund.html)), and Plasma Pong showed that a fluid can carry a two-player game and, when Atari's trademark objection pulled it in 2007, that names in this genre carry legal risk ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_Pong)). Licences decide what reference code can be reused: only MIT-licensed projects such as Butterchurn can go into closed code with nothing but attribution, while projectM is LGPL-2.1 and the Particle Life desktop app GPL-3.0 ([Butterchurn](https://github.com/jberg/butterchurn/blob/master/LICENSE); [projectM](https://github.com/projectM-visualizer/projectm/blob/master/COPYING); [Particle Life](https://github.com/tom-mohr/particle-life-app/blob/main/LICENSE.md)). Nobody has yet reviewed Uzu's preset names, screenshots or in-app screens, so that review and a trademark search should precede any naming or interface freeze.

| Uzu element to leave alone | What Tactilume owns instead |
|---|---|
| "Uzu", swirl or whirlpool puns, "Colordodge", both subtitles, the "kinetic multitouch particle visualizer" tagline | An original name, cleared by a trademark search |
| One global mode ladder chosen by 0–10 simultaneous touches, its mode names, auto-play that adds virtual touches | Per-player tools and curated worlds, plus a generic drift or attract mode |
| The 55 and 67 factory presets, a "Legacy Presets" folder, the same ten-effect bundle in the same order, "Low to Ultra" labels | Worlds, scene codes, community presets and "Auto (this machine)" quality |
| The 2010 yellow-green-red palette, three-armed spirals and springy ribbons as hero looks | HDR light, fluids, flocks and particle-life ecologies |
| Its press blurbs, accolade list and external-display party pitch | Several players, capture and sharing, comfort features |

## Desktop GPUs stand still until 2027, so tiers key on bandwidth

The installed base will barely move before Tactilume ships. NVIDIA reportedly releases no new RTX gaming GPUs in 2026, with the RTX 50 Super expected at CES 2027 at the earliest and the RTX 60 around 2028 ([Tom's Hardware](https://www.tomshardware.com/pc-components/gpus/report-claims-nvidia-will-not-be-releasing-any-new-rtx-gaming-gpus-in-2026-rtx-60-series-likely-debuting-in-2028)), and AMD's RDNA 5 is expected in the second half of 2027 ([PCGH](https://www.pcgameshardware.de/Grafikkarten-Grafikkarte-97980/News/GPU-Rumors-RDNA-5-RTX-50-Super-Intel-Arc-Analysis-1545000/)). Valve's own tables for the October 2026 Steam survey could not be opened, so the figures below come from secondary reports and are unverified.

| Top GPUs ([TweakTown](https://www.tweaktown.com/news/114454/rtx-5070-loses-its-steam-gpu-crown-after-just-one-month-as-rtx-3060-returns-to-the-top/index.html)) | RTX 3060 3.62%, RTX 5070 3.56%, with the top six within 0.65 points |

The typical Steam PC, with Windows 11, six to eight cores, 16–32 GB of RAM, an 8–16 GB RTX 3060- or 4060-class card and a 1080p screen, should anchor **Recommended, not Minimum**, because a light toy's audience leans towards integrated-graphics laptops and Macs. The survey swings by several points a month with its respondents' language mix, so tier decisions should use three-to-six-month averages. Displays are changing faster than GPUs: OLED monitor shipments grew **98% year on year in Q2 2026**, with more than 4 million forecast for the year and 27-inch 1440p at 240 Hz as the growth segment ([TrendForce](https://www.trendforce.com/insights/oled-monitor)), while OLED is still only about 6% of laptop panels ([TrendForce](https://www.trendforce.com/presscenter/news/20261029-13287.html)). Nobody publishes how many players have high-refresh or HDR screens, and DisplayHDR 400 looks close to SDR next to DisplayHDR 1000's 1,000 nits and 0.05-nit black ([VESA](https://displayhdr.org/)), so SDR should stay the default look, with HDR offered where the display supports it.

For a particle toy simulated on the GPU and drawn with additive blending, **memory bandwidth and graphics memory are better tier keys than FP32 TFLOPS**. Paper TFLOPS mislead across vendors: AMD's dual-issue figures roughly double the useful rate, and the Radeon 780M is only 5–10% faster than the 680M despite about twice the paper number ([UltrabookReview](https://www.ultrabookreview.com/64396-amd-radeon-780m-benchmarks)). Integrated-GPU bandwidth follows the laptop's RAM, so two laptops with the same "780M" can differ by about 30%, and Apple's GPUs blend inside on-chip tile memory, so blending traffic largely disappears on Macs ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/metal/tailor-your-apps-for-apple-gpus-and-tile-based-deferred-rendering)). The notes' budget rule is that **particles × bytes per particle per frame × refresh rate must stay below 0.4 × peak bandwidth minus resolution overhead**, where 0.4 combines an assumed 80% achievable bandwidth with half of each frame given to particles; neither factor has a source, so the app must calibrate both. The tiers below follow from that rule, with every reference GPU meant "or equivalent" and every particle count modelled rather than measured.

| Tier | GPU floor (bandwidth, FP32, memory) | Reference GPUs | Display target | Particles (modelled) | CPU, RAM, OS |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Minimum | ≥60 GB/s, 2 TFLOPS, 2 GB (shared is fine) | Intel Iris Xe, Radeon 680M/780M, Apple M1, GTX 1650 | 1080p or 1200p at 60 Hz, SDR | ~1–2M | 4 cores; 8 GB (16 GB with integrated graphics); Windows 10 22H2 64-bit, macOS 13+ on Apple silicon, or 64-bit Linux with Vulkan |
| Recommended | ≥250 GB/s, 10 TFLOPS, 8 GB | RTX 3060/4060, RX 7600/9060 XT, Arc B580, Apple M4/M5 Pro, Radeon 8060S | 1080p at 144 Hz or 1440p at 60 Hz | ~5–10M | 6 cores/12 threads; 16 GB; Windows 11 |
| High | ≥440 GB/s, 20 TFLOPS, 12 GB | RTX 5070, RX 9070 XT, RTX 5060 Ti 16 GB, Apple M4/M5 Max | 1440p at 144–240 Hz, HDR optional | ~10–15M | 8 cores/16 threads; 16–32 GB |
| Ultra | ≥880 GB/s, 40 TFLOPS, 16 GB | RTX 5080, RTX 5090 (RTX 5070 Ti entry) | 4K at 120–144 Hz or 1440p at 360–480 Hz; OLED or DisplayHDR 1000 | ~20–50M+ | 8+ cores; 32 GB |

At a typical 128 bytes of memory traffic per particle per frame, the rule gives **about 2.5 million particles for an Iris Xe at 1080p60**, close to the mobile edition's 2 million; about 4.9 million for an RTX 4060 at 1080p144; about 12.7 million for an RTX 5070 at 1440p144 but only 6.9 million at 1440p240; and about 35 million for an RTX 5090 at 4K144. **Fifty million particles at 240 Hz fit on no single GPU.** The fact-check recomputed this arithmetic, but the peak bandwidths behind it come from spec aggregators and [NVIDIA's comparison page](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/geforce/graphics-cards/compare/), unverified at vendor level. Blending can exhaust bandwidth before simulation does: on NVIDIA, AMD and Intel GPUs, a million 64-pixel glow sprites at 60 Hz move about 61 GB/s, more than an Iris Xe's whole usable budget, so **particle count, sprite size and glow radius, and internal render scale must be separate quality settings**. The light-rendering notes, assuming 16-byte packed state and one kernel that both simulates and draws, estimate 10–30 million particles at 240 Hz on RTX 5070-class cards; the gap between that and the 6.9 million above is the gap between data layouts, which makes **bytes per particle the single largest lever in the engine**. None of these numbers has been measured on a real GPU.

Comparable creative and simulation titles name one or two reference GPUs "or equivalent", state graphics memory, add a line about integrated graphics and give an API floor, as Universe Sandbox ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/230290/Universe_Sandbox/)), Teardown ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1167630)) and Wallpaper Engine ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/431960/Wallpaper_Engine/)) do. Tactilume's store page should add "a WebGPU-capable GPU (DirectX 12, Vulkan or Metal)", a footnote that laptops with switchable graphics must run it on the discrete GPU, and an honest Linux line, "Intel Gen12+ (Mesa 22+) or NVIDIA (driver 535.183.01+) for WebGPU; other GPUs run the WebGL2 renderer", because Chromium's Linux blocklist exempts only those two ([Chromium](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chromium/chromium/156.0.8078.96/gpu/config/software_rendering_list.json)). Electron 45 itself needs Windows 10 or macOS 13 Ventura or later ([Electron](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/electron/electron/v45.3.0/README.md)), and with Windows 10 still on about a fifth of Steam PCs, supporting 22H2 at Minimum, labelled as unsupported by Microsoft, costs little. Whether the AMD-based Steam Deck and Steam Machine are in scope is a product decision the two sets of notes disagree on.

## "Auto (this machine)" makes the user's own system the default

Making a system like the user's own the default has a two-layer answer. The public Minimum-to-Ultra floors stay anchored to market data, so that a developer's high-end card never becomes the advertised Recommended spec, while **the default preset on every machine is "Auto (this machine)": the tier the machine measures itself into, with its own particle count, sprite size and render scale**. The developer's own machine is the first preset to tune, and Minimum is tested on an Iris Xe or Radeon 780M laptop. The research did not record the user's hardware, so the same detector and benchmark, plus a short questionnaire, place it.

Detection takes about 100 ms and combines several sources. Electron's `app.getGPUInfo('basic')` returns every GPU's PCI vendor and device IDs with an `active` flag, plus hybrid-laptop flags and the machine model ([Electron](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/electron/electron/main/docs/api/app.md)). WebGPU's own `adapter.info` carries only vendor, architecture, subgroup sizes and a software-fallback flag by default, leaving the device ID and description empty unless a developer flag is set ([Chromium](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chromium/chromium/main/third_party/blink/renderer/modules/webgpu/gpu_adapter.cc)), and Dawn, Chrome's WebGPU engine, derives architecture names such as `lovelace`, `rdna-3` or `xe-2-lpg` from PCI-ID ranges, while Apple reports only a Metal family such as `common-3` ([Dawn](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/dawn/main/src/dawn/gpu_info.json)). Electron's `Display` gives refresh rate, colour depth and whether a panel is built in ([Electron](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/electron/electron/main/docs/api/structures/display.md)), `powerMonitor` reports battery, thermal state and CPU speed limits ([Electron](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/electron/electron/main/docs/api/power-monitor.md)), and `process.getSystemMemoryInfo()` gives exact RAM ([Electron](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/electron/electron/main/docs/api/process.md)). WebGPU limits are deliberately coarse, since browsers may expose no more than 32 distinguishable capability buckets ([WebGPU spec](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/main/spec/index.bs)), so they serve as hard caps, never as a speed measure; the PCI IDs instead feed a local table of bandwidth, real FP32 rate and memory that supplies the starting estimate. The developer flag that would fill in the adapter description should not ship, because it adds fingerprinting surface and Electron's PCI IDs make it unnecessary.

| Step | Time | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| Spec prior | Instant | Tier floor from the PCI-ID table; unknown and Apple GPUs start at Minimum unless the chip variant is known |
| Bandwidth probe | ~1 s | Time a 256 MB buffer copy to calibrate achievable bandwidth |
| Particle ramp | 10–15 s after a 2–3 s warm-up | At native resolution and refresh, start at half the prior's budget and multiply by 1.4 every 1–1.5 s until 90th-percentile GPU time exceeds 75% of the refresh period (5.2 ms at 144 Hz) or a memory cap, then bisect |
| Soak | 15–20 s at 85% | Cut 15% and flag the machine as heat-limited if the last 3 s run more than 10% slower, the OS speed limit drops below 100, or thermal state reaches "serious" |
| Profile | Stored | Highest tier whose target fits, keyed by GPU IDs, driver, display, refresh and power source; re-run after GPU, display or power changes and after device loss |

The measurement overrides the prior in both directions. Shipping builds round WebGPU timestamps to steps of about 65.5 µs ([Dawn](https://github.com/google/dawn/blob/main/src/dawn/common/Constants.h)), fine for whole-frame timing but too coarse for individual passes, and because laptops slow as they heat over minutes, a runtime governor should shed particles or render scale whenever frames stay over budget for more than two seconds. Hybrid laptops are the main trap. **Chrome ignores `powerPreference` on Windows** and uses the GPU it was allocated, which on laptops "is generally the integrated graphics card" ([Chromium](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chromium/chromium/main/third_party/blink/renderer/modules/webgpu/gpu.cc)), and WebGPU follows the adapter of Chrome's whole GPU process, so nothing short of moving that process helps ([Chromium](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chromium/chromium/156.0.8078.96/gpu/command_buffer/service/webgpu_decoder_impl.cc)). When `getGPUInfo` shows an inactive discrete GPU beside an active integrated one, the app should prompt once for Windows Settings → Graphics → High performance and re-benchmark; Chromium's own `force-high-performance-gpu` switch exists but is untested. Apple silicon has a single GPU ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/metal/mtldevice/islowpower)), but placing a Mac needs the chip variant and GPU-core count, which WebGPU hides. A diagnostics button can fill in nearly everything and ask the user only for what software cannot read: the laptop GPU's power limit, whether it has a MUX switch, the monitor's HDR tier and the Apple GPU-core count.

This report recommends one further step. Because profiles are keyed by GPU, driver, display, refresh and power source, the developer's measured profile can ship as the prior for that hardware class, so **any machine like the user's starts from a measured tier rather than a modelled one**, and the ramp merely confirms it. Over time, anonymous opt-in calibration results from players can replace the unsourced 0.8 and 0.5 factors in the budget rule with fleet data that the research could not find anywhere.

## WebGPU in Electron 45 delivers compute and HDR, not ray tracing

WebGPU is on by default in Chrome and Electron on **Windows through Direct3D 12 for NVIDIA, AMD and Intel, and on macOS through Metal**, with Qualcomm's Windows-on-Arm GPUs gated by driver version ([gpuweb](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/wiki/Implementation-Status); [Chromium](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/gpu/config/webgpu_blocklist_impl.cc)). Linux is the exception: Chromium 156 exempts only **Intel Gen12+ on Mesa 22.0 or newer and NVIDIA on driver 535.183.01 or newer** from the blocklist entry that disables WebGPU there, so AMD users, including the Steam Deck and the Steam Machine, get no WebGPU by default ([Chromium](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chromium/chromium/156.0.8078.96/gpu/config/software_rendering_list.json)). The narrowest override is the `ForceEnableWebGpuInterop` feature, which opens only that entry and keeps Chromium's "safe" level, whereas `--enable-unsafe-webgpu` turns off the whole adapter blocklist and its protection against known-bad drivers ([Chromium](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/156.0.8078.96/gpu/config/gpu_util.cc)); neither has been tested on AMD's open-source RADV driver. Software-only adapters in virtual machines and remote sessions are always blocked ([Chromium](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chromium/chromium/main/gpu/config/webgpu_blocklist_impl.cc)), one more reason to keep WebGL2. Electron 45 (45.3.x) pins Chromium 156.0.8078.96, and since Chrome now ships a milestone every two weeks while Electron keeps an eight-week cycle, each Electron major jumps four Chromium versions ([Chromium](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/docs/process/release_cycle.md); [Electron](https://github.com/electron/electron/blob/45-x-y/DEPS)). The app should therefore detect features at runtime rather than key on versions, and plan to move up a major every 8–16 weeks; Electron 46, in alpha on Chromium 159 ([npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/electron)), will bring linear canvas colour spaces and 64-bit atomics by default.

Memory limits must be requested when the device is created. The spec's defaults of 128 MiB per storage binding and 256 MiB per buffer would quietly cap particles near the mobile edition's scale, but **every Windows adapter offers a 2 GiB buffer and a binding of 2 GiB minus 4 bytes** because Dawn assumes 2 GiB on Direct3D 12 ([Dawn](https://github.com/google/dawn/blob/main/src/dawn/common/Constants.h)), NVIDIA's Vulkan driver is capped at the same size after a buffer-length bug ([Dawn](https://github.com/google/dawn/blob/main/src/dawn/native/vulkan/PhysicalDeviceVk.cpp)), and only Metal and non-NVIDIA Vulkan reach 4 GiB. At 32 bytes per particle one 2 GiB binding holds about 67 million particles, and 50 million need about 1.5 GiB per copy. Every device keeps the limit of 65,535 workgroups per dispatch dimension ([Dawn](https://github.com/google/dawn/blob/main/src/dawn/native/Limits.cpp)), so 50 million particles at 256 threads per workgroup need a 2D dispatch. WGSL still has **no floating-point atomics**, only 32-bit integer ones plus an optional 64-bit minimum and maximum ([WGSL](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/blob/main/wgsl/index.bs)); Electron 45 exposes that 64-bit feature only behind a developer flag, and M1 Macs lack the hardware for it ([Chromium](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/156.0.8078.96/gpu/command_buffer/service/webgpu_decoder_impl.cc)). Half-precision shaders, subgroup operations and 64-bit atomics must all stay optional code paths.

HDR is reachable today. A half-float (`rgba16float`) canvas configured with **`toneMapping: { mode: "extended" }` shows values brighter than SDR white**, in Chrome 129+ on Windows and macOS and 144+ on Linux, though not in Firefox or Safari ([BCD](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/GPUCanvasContext.json)), and browsers without tone mapping leave it out of `getConfiguration()`, which doubles as the feature test ([WebGPU spec](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/blob/main/spec/index.bs)). Two details bite in Electron 45. The linear `srgb-linear` canvas colour space is still experimental in Chromium 156 and stable only from 157 ([Chromium 156](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/156.0.8078.96/third_party/blink/renderer/platform/runtime_enabled_features.json5); [Chromium 157](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/157.0.8103.81/third_party/blink/renderer/platform/runtime_enabled_features.json5)), so the final pass must write sRGB-encoded values, with four times SDR white written as about 1.82 rather than 4.0. And **no API reports the display's peak brightness or SDR-white level**, only the yes-or-no `dynamic-range` media query, which reports capability rather than whether HDR is switched on ([W3C Color on the Web CG](https://github.com/w3c/ColorWeb-CG/blob/main/hdr_html_canvas_element.md)), so the app needs an in-game calibration screen for peak brightness and paper white. PQ and HLG canvases remain experimental, Chromium's HDR display features are ChromeOS-only so Linux users should expect SDR ([Chromium](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/components/viz/common/features.cc)), and real HDR output has not yet been seen on any OS.

High refresh rates work, but without controls. Chromium paces frames from the display's refresh signal, through DXGI or the DirectComposition clock on Windows and DisplayLink on macOS ([Chromium](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/ui/gl/vsync_thread_win.cc)), and the WebGPU canvas has **no option for present mode, latency or variable refresh rate, and no presentation-timing feedback** ([WebGPU spec](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/blob/main/spec/index.bs)). Chromium's own source notes that variable refresh is not implemented on Windows, and its `--disable-frame-rate-limit` switch also turns off vsync, so it suits benchmarks only ([Chromium](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/components/viz/common/switches.cc)). The engine therefore runs a fixed-timestep simulation decoupled from the display, interpolates when rendering, measures the real frame interval and keeps at most two frames queued, within a GPU budget of 8.3 ms at 120 Hz, 4.2 ms at 240 Hz and 2 ms at 500 Hz; above 240 Hz, the simulation stays at 120 or 240 Hz and only rendering follows the panel.

**Hardware ray tracing does not exist in WebGPU**: it is missing from the spec's 25 optional features, from Dawn's API definition and from Chrome, even experimentally ([WebGPU spec](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/blob/main/spec/index.bs); [Dawn](https://github.com/google/dawn/blob/main/src/dawn/dawn.json)). The native Rust library wgpu has experimental ray queries and ray-tracing pipelines at version 30, with Metal acceleration structures since version 29 and its newest additions limited to Vulkan ([wgpu](https://github.com/gfx-rs/wgpu/blob/trunk/CHANGELOG.md)), but using it would mean a native module with no shared texture path into the WebGPU canvas, so Tactilume's ray-traced looks must come from compute shaders, as the light-rendering section explains. Robustness needs design too. A lost device must be rebuilt from a fresh adapter, because adapters are single-use ([WebGPU spec](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/blob/main/spec/index.bs)); Chrome's GPU watchdog fires after 30 s on Windows, 25 s on macOS and 15 s elsewhere ([Chromium](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/gpu/ipc/common/gpu_watchdog_timeout.h)); and the app should keep each submission short, rebuild after a loss from a compact snapshot of emitter and preset settings rather than gigabytes of particles, and drop to WebGL2 after repeated losses.

## Electron keeps one engine on every OS, which Tauri cannot

Electron 45 shipped on 21 October 2026 with Chromium 156, V8 15.6 and Node 24.22 ([Electron](https://github.com/electron/website/blob/main/blog/electron-45-0.md)), adds **121–158 MB of compressed runtime** per platform ([GitHub release assets](https://github.com/electron/electron/releases/expanded_assets/v45.3.0)), and runs on Windows 10+ and macOS 13+ with x64 and arm64 builds for all three systems. Each alternative breaks something Tactilume needs. Tauri 2 relies on each OS's own web view, and WebKitGTK on Linux has no WebGPU build option even at 2.54 ([WebKit](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/WebKit/WebKit/webkitglib/2.54/Source/cmake/OptionsGTK.cmake)), the macOS web view gets WebGPU only on macOS 26+ ([Tauri](https://github.com/tauri-apps/tauri/issues/6381)), and WebHID exists only in Windows' WebView2. NW.js 0.119 also bundles Chromium, but the research did not check its versions, tooling or signing path ([npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/nw)), and an installable web app cannot listen on the local network for phones at all. Native code, whether Rust with wgpu or SDL3 over Vulkan, Direct3D 12 and Metal, is the only route to hardware ray tracing, to DLSS through NVIDIA Streamline, which needs DirectX 11 or Vulkan 1.2 ([NVIDIA](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NVIDIA-RTX/Streamline/main/README.md)), to Intel's XeSS 3 upscaling, frame generation and low-latency mode ([Intel](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/intel/xess/main/README.md)), and to SDL3's built-in gyro, touchpad, trigger-rumble and LED support ([SDL](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/libsdl-org/SDL/main/include/SDL3/SDL_gamepad.h)), at the price of rewriting the WebGL2 renderer and JavaScript bundle, writing a separate server and losing the web developer tools. The sensible middle path is **Electron for version 1, revisiting native code only if ray tracing or vendor upscalers become product requirements**.

The shell should follow Electron's own security checklist ([Electron](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/electron/electron/main/docs/tutorial/security.md)): one sandboxed, context-isolated window with a typed preload bridge; the app served from an `app://` custom protocol rather than `file://`, under a `default-src 'self'` content policy; navigation and `window.open` denied; and build-time fuses that turn off `runAsNode`, `nodeOptions`, `nodeCliInspect` and extra `file://` privileges while turning on archive integrity checks ([Electron](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/electron/electron/main/docs/tutorial/fuses.md)). The phone server belongs in a separate `utilityProcess` that passes decoded input to the renderer over a `MessagePort` ([Electron](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/electron/electron/main/docs/api/utility-process.md)), listens only while pairing or phone control is on, checks `Origin` and `Host` on every WebSocket connection, validates and rate-limits every message, and never lets network input reach privileged IPC.

Operating-system network prompts shape the protocol. On macOS 15+, **listening for and accepting incoming TCP needs no Local Network permission**, but outgoing TCP, any UDP send, multicast reception and every Bonjour operation do, through every API, and apps launched from Terminal are allowed automatically, so developers never see the prompt their users will ([Apple TN3179](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/technotes/tn3179-understanding-local-network-privacy)). A desktop that only accepts phone connections over HTTP and WebSocket therefore raises no macOS prompt. The phone-link design below, however, adds a WebRTC data channel, UDP for the Android app and a Bonjour advertisement for Android's device picker, and by Apple's rule each of these needs the permission. This report recommends asking for it when a user first turns on phone control, with an `NSLocalNetworkUsageDescription` string, whose absence developers report causes silent denial ([GitHub issue](https://github.com/getpaseo/paseo/issues/2880), search summary only), and keeping a TCP-only path that still works if the user declines. On Windows, an all-users installer can create a firewall rule limited to private networks and the local subnet; per-user installs cannot, so the app should notice when no phone has connected and explain how to mark the network as Private.

Code signing is a fixed cost worth paying. Apple's Developer Program costs **US$99 a year** ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/programs/whats-included/)), every macOS build should be notarized with `notarytool` under the hardened runtime ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/security/notarizing-macos-software-before-distribution)) because macOS Sequoia removed the Control-click route around Gatekeeper ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/news/?id=saqachfa)), and the build keeps the audio-input entitlement and adds `NSAudioCaptureUsageDescription` for system audio. Microsoft's April 2026 guidance lists the Windows options ([Microsoft](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MicrosoftDocs/windows-dev-docs/docs/hub/apps/package-and-deploy/code-signing-options.md)):

| Windows signing option | Cost | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Microsoft Store (MSIX) | Free | Microsoft re-signs the package |
| Azure Artifact Signing | ~US$9.99 a month | Organisations in the US, Canada, EU and UK; individuals only in the US and Canada |
| OV certificate | US$150–300 a year | Keys on hardware since June 2023 |
| EV certificate | US$400+ a year | Same SmartScreen treatment as OV since 2024 |

Electron's own docs still say EV is required, but Microsoft's newer guidance should win, and even signed apps show as "unrecognized" until they build reputation over "several weeks and hundreds of clean installs" ([Microsoft](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MicrosoftDocs/windows-dev-docs/docs/hub/apps/package-and-deploy/smartscreen-reputation.md)). For direct distribution, electron-builder can produce an NSIS installer, a notarized DMG plus ZIP and an AppImage, kept current by `electron-updater` from GitHub Releases ([electron-builder](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/electron-userland/electron-builder/master/packages/electron-updater/README.md)); a Flatpak needs `--device=input` (Flatpak 1.15.6+) to see game controllers ([Flatpak](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/flatpak/flatpak-docs/master/docs/sandbox-permissions.rst)), and AppImages need FUSE 2, packaged as `libfuse2t64` on Ubuntu 24.04 ([AppImage](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/AppImage/docs.appimage.org/master/source/user-guide/troubleshooting/fuse.rst)). Update checks must fail silently offline, and a build marker should switch in-app updates off for Steam, Store and Flatpak builds.

Steam is feasible but fragile. The usual binding, steamworks.js, last published 0.4.0 in August 2024 and has no Windows or Linux arm64 builds ([npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/steamworks.js)); its overlay helper forces Chromium's in-process GPU mode, disables DirectComposition and adds a 60 Hz repaint timer ([steamworks.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ceifa/steamworks.js/main/index.js)), and the overlay works on Windows only if Steam starts before the app is ready and stays broken on macOS ([issue #102](https://github.com/ceifa/steamworks.js/issues/102)). In-process GPU mode removes the crash isolation a GPU-heavy toy relies on, so the overlay should be an optional switch in Steam builds only. Automated testing splits in two, because GitHub's hosted runners have no GPUs ([GitHub](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/actions/runner-images/main/README.md)). In the research team's own headless test, a software WebGPU adapter ran compute, offscreen rendering and buffer readback correctly but could not present a canvas, even though `configure()` succeeded, which fits Chromium's Linux presentation path being disabled under software rendering ([Chromium](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/156.0.8078.96/gpu/config/gpu_util.cc)). Hosted runners can therefore check shader compilation, compute results against CPU references and offscreen frames against tolerance-based reference images on every push, while presentation, frame pacing, HDR, multi-monitor setups, physical gamepads and the Steam overlay need a real-GPU set before each release: a Windows PC with a discrete GPU, an Apple-silicon Mac and a Linux AMD machine. The same finding defines the app's fallback check: present a few frames at startup with a WebGPU promise pending and treat a rejection or lost device within a timeout as failure, because a successful `configure()` proves nothing.

## Phones join over one protocol split by the secure-context line

The phone link has two client routes and one protocol whose messages are the same bytes on every transport. Browser phones, meaning any iPhone and Android phones without the app, load the controller page from the desktop's own server and connect back to it. A WebSocket works everywhere, including `ws://` from a plain-HTTP page, but one lost packet delays every later message; the Node `ws` library at least turns off Nagle's algorithm ([ws](https://github.com/websockets/ws/blob/8.22.2/lib/websocket.js)). For the continuous touch and motion stream, **a WebRTC data channel set to `{ordered: false, maxRetransmits: 0}` drops stale samples instead of stalling**, exists in iOS Safari, Chrome for Android and Android WebView, and is not limited to secure pages ([MDN](https://github.com/mdn/content/blob/main/files/en-us/web/security/defenses/secure_contexts/features_restricted_to_secure_contexts/index.md); [W3C](https://github.com/w3c/webrtc-pc/blob/main/webrtc.html)). On a home network without a STUN server the desktop must advertise its raw IP addresses, because Safari shows its own addresses only as random `.local` names and ignores remote `.local` names not in that form ([WebKit](https://github.com/WebKit/WebKit/blob/main/Source/WebCore/Modules/mediastream/PeerConnectionBackend.cpp)); `node-datachannel`, MPL-2.0 with prebuilt binaries for all three desktop OSes, serves the desktop end ([npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/node-datachannel)), though neither phone browser has been tested against it. WebTransport now ships in every engine, Safari since 26.4, but requires a secure page, certificates valid for at most two weeks and a native HTTP/3 add-on that Node's experimental QUIC module does not replace ([W3C](https://github.com/w3c/webtransport/blob/main/index.bs); [Node](https://github.com/nodejs/node/blob/main/doc/api/quic.md)), so it is deferred, and Web Bluetooth is absent on iOS and ruled out ([BCD](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/Bluetooth.json)).

Whether the page is a "secure context" decides what a browser controller can do. An `http://192.168.x.x` page is not one ([MDN](https://github.com/mdn/content/blob/main/files/en-us/web/security/defenses/secure_contexts/index.md)), so it **loses tilt and motion entirely**, since Safari 26.4 and Chromium no longer even expose the motion interfaces there ([Chromium](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/modules/device_orientation/device_motion_event.idl); [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/safari-release-notes/safari-26_4-release-notes)), along with full-rate touch samples, the screen-wake lock, the cryptography API, WebTransport and WebCodecs, while keeping touch and pointer events, predicted samples, WebSocket, WebRTC and vibration. A trusted HTTPS page needs the Plex model, in which a vendor domain gives each install a name resolving to the desktop's private address, backed by a wildcard certificate obtained through a DNS challenge. That needs internet and breaks behind routers that filter DNS answers pointing at private addresses, a failure the browser makers' Local Network Access proposal itself acknowledges ([WICG](https://github.com/WICG/local-network-access/blob/main/explainer.md)), and Let's Encrypt's limit of 50 certificates per registered domain per week means the vendor domain must join the Public Suffix List ([Let's Encrypt](https://github.com/letsencrypt/website/blob/main/content/en/docs/rate-limits.md)). **A plain-HTTP, touch-only mode is therefore unavoidable offline**, and the phone should say so. Browser local-network rules do not get in the way, because requests from a page loaded from a home-network address back to that address are local-to-local, which the proposal exempts ([WICG](https://github.com/WICG/local-network-access/blob/main/explainer.md)), Chrome does not yet apply its checks to WebRTC ([Chromium](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/157.0.8103.81/services/network/public/cpp/features.cc)), and iOS exempts Safari and in-app web views from its Local Network permission ([Apple TN3179](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/technotes/tn3179-understanding-local-network-privacy)). Hosting the controller on a public website instead would run into permission prompts and mixed-content blocking.

The Tactilume Android app avoids most of these compromises, but Android 17 changed the rules. For apps targeting SDK 37, the local network **"is blocked by default": TCP in both directions, UDP and `.local` lookups all fail through every API**, with TCP timing out and UDP returning a permission error, unless the app holds the new `ACCESS_LOCAL_NETWORK` permission or the user picks the desktop in NsdManager's system picker, a grant that needs no permission and survives reboots ([Android](https://developer.android.com/privacy-and-security/local-network-permission)). Networking belongs in Kotlin rather than in the app's web page: the page's `https://appassets.androidplatform.net` origin is secure, which suits full-rate touches and motion, but `ws://` from it is blocked as both mixed content and cleartext ([Android SDK 37.2 sources](https://dl.google.com/android/repository/source-37.2_r01.zip)). The recommended app transport is **TLS over TCP pinned to the desktop's self-signed key for control, plus UDP authenticated with a key from that TLS session for the realtime stream**, after a one-time six-digit code derived from both TLS fingerprints. Native sensors run at up to 200 Hz without a special permission, so a 100 Hz orientation stream is easy, alongside native vibration effects, orientation lock and immersive mode. Android browsers fall under the same permission once they target SDK 37; Chrome 157 still targets 36, and whether a page loaded from a home-network address then triggers a prompt is untested.

Latency is where phones are weakest, and nobody has measured it rigorously for phone browsers on a home network. A 2014 study found that people can tell 1 ms from 2 ms of lag when dragging a stylus, while commercial devices of the time took 65–120 ms end to end ([CHI 2014](https://webdocs.cs.ualberta.ca/~wfb/publications/C-2014-SIGCHI-Latency.pdf)), so any phone-to-screen path will be noticeable; Wi-Fi power saving can add hundreds of milliseconds in the worst case ([USPTO 9210656](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9210656), search summary only), and AirConsole, the best-known phone-controller platform, targets under 100 ms ([AirConsole](https://developers.airconsole.com/best-practices-for-web-games-on-airconsole), search summary only). A finger-driven light toy should aim for 20–40 ms and hide the rest with prediction and with instant local ripples on the phone itself. The protocol stamps every sample at its source, syncs clocks every 2–5 seconds from the samples with the shortest round trip, the game-industry method the `timesync` library implements ([npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/timesync)), and plays input out through an adaptive 0–25 ms jitter buffer with interpolation and at most about 1.5 frames of extrapolation. Packed binary messages, with an 8-byte header, 8-byte touch samples and 12 bytes of motion, put ten fingers plus motion with double redundancy at about 180 bytes, or **about 22 KB/s per phone at 120 Hz**, so ten phones need under 3 Mbit/s. Haptics differ sharply by route: Android browsers can vibrate only right after a touch ([BCD](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/Navigator.json)), iPhone pages get haptics only as a side effect of real taps on an HTML switch ([ios-haptics](https://github.com/tijnjh/ios-haptics/blob/main/README.md)), and the app has full vibration control, so the desktop sends meaningful cues ("tick", "thud") that each phone plays as best it can. Pairing uses a QR code carrying the HTTPS address and a 128-bit join code in the URL fragment, with a join notice on the big screen and a PIN as fallback.

## Gamepads add two analog hands each, and WebHID adds the gyro

The standard Gamepad API is the base layer for every controller, and in Electron 45 it is generous: **up to 8 pads with 16 axes and 32 buttons each** ([Chromium](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/156.0.8078.96/device/gamepad/public/cpp/gamepads.h)), read by Chromium every 4 ms ([Chromium](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/156.0.8078.96/device/gamepad/gamepad_provider.cc)) and by the page once per frame, and on Windows not even subject to XInput's four-pad limit, because that backend is not registered ([Chromium](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/156.0.8078.96/device/gamepad/gamepad_platform_data_fetcher.h)). Pads stay invisible until a button press, which doubles as a "press any button to join" screen, updates stop while the window is hidden, and from Chromium 159, which Electron 46 will carry, a press counts as a user action only when the window also has focus, which matters for flows that open WebHID from a button press. The API has **no gyro, accelerometer, lights, adaptive triggers or battery level on any OS** ([BCD](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/Gamepad.json)), and its rumble is coarse: fixed duration, constant strength from 0 to 1 and at most 5 s per call ([Chromium](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/156.0.8078.96/third_party/blink/renderer/modules/gamepad/gamepad_haptic_actuator.cc)). Trigger rumble is effectively Xbox-on-Windows only, and on Windows the DualSense gets no rumble at all through this API, because Chromium's Windows haptics code covers only the DualShock 4 ([Chromium](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/156.0.8078.96/device/gamepad/raw_input_gamepad_device_win.cc)). An experimental event-driven input mode can be enabled, but should be feature-detected with per-frame polling as the fallback; polling at 60 Hz adds about 8 ms of average latency ([Microsoft Edge explainer](https://github.com/MicrosoftEdge/MSEdgeExplainers/blob/main/GamepadEventDrivenInputAPI/explainer.md)).

WebHID, which gives web code raw access to HID devices, supplies what the Gamepad API lacks for PlayStation and Nintendo controllers. Chromium's HID blocklist names no Sony, Nintendo, Microsoft or Valve device and does not protect gamepads ([Chromium](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/156.0.8078.96/services/device/public/cpp/hid/hid_blocklist.cc)), but Electron provides no device chooser: the app must handle `select-hid-device`, call `preventDefault()` and pick the device itself, and can re-grant known controllers silently with `setDevicePermissionHandler` ([Electron](https://github.com/electron/electron/blob/v45.3.0/docs/tutorial/devices.md)), because without `preventDefault()` the request quietly returns nothing ([Electron](https://github.com/electron/electron/blob/v45.3.0/shell/browser/hid/hid_chooser_controller.cc)). The DualSense then yields **a gyro at 1,024 units per degree per second (±2,048 °/s), an accelerometer at 8,192 units per g, a two-finger 1920×1080 touchpad, rumble, adaptive triggers, the light bar and player lights** ([Linux hid-playstation](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/master/drivers/hid/hid-playstation.c)), with sensors reporting at 250 Hz over USB and about 1,000 Hz over Bluetooth ([SDL](https://github.com/libsdl-org/SDL/blob/main/src/joystick/hidapi/SDL_hidapi_ps5.c)). Switch Pro and Joy-Con controllers deliver a 200 Hz motion sensor but need their commands spaced 20 ms apart over USB and 60 ms over Bluetooth ([Linux hid-nintendo](https://github.com/torvalds/linux/blob/master/drivers/hid/hid-nintendo.c)). Switch 2 controllers need a non-HID USB start-up step and are unsupported over Bluetooth even in SDL ([SDL](https://github.com/libsdl-org/SDL/blob/main/src/joystick/hidapi/SDL_hidapi_switch2.c)), and Xbox pads over USB are not HID devices, so neither gets a WebHID plan. Linux needs device permissions, which Valve's udev rules already grant for most of these pads ([Valve](https://github.com/ValveSoftware/steam-devices/blob/master/60-steam-input.rules)). The best-known DualSense library is LGPL-3.0, so a closed app must ship it as a replaceable module or write its own parser, while the DualShock 4 and Joy-Con libraries are MIT and Apache-2.0 ([npm dualsense-ts](https://registry.npmjs.org/dualsense-ts); [npm webhid-ds4](https://registry.npmjs.org/webhid-ds4); [npm joy-con-webhid](https://registry.npmjs.org/joy-con-webhid)). Each controller needs exactly one haptics owner, because Gamepad API rumble and WebHID commands overwrite each other on a DualSense.

Steam Input complicates this whenever Steam is running, because it presents controllers as a **"Steam Virtual Gamepad" that Chromium treats as an Xbox 360 pad**, with a standard button layout but no gyro, touchpad, lights or adaptive triggers ([SDL](https://github.com/libsdl-org/SDL/blob/main/src/joystick/controller_list.h); [Chromium](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/156.0.8078.96/device/gamepad/gamepad_id_list.cc)). Since gyro and touch are core verbs, Tactilume should default to the native Gamepad API plus WebHID for PlayStation and Switch pads, accept Steam's virtual pad for Steam Controllers, the Steam Deck and Switch 2 pads, and detect the virtual pad to warn about double input; how Steam Input interacts with WebHID inside an Electron build is still unknown.

Gyro aiming has settled practice, codified in Jibb Smart's JoyShockMapper: calibrate the sensor's drift while the pad is still and offer recalibration, turn rotation speed into cursor speed at a sensitivity of 8 or more for an on-screen cursor, work in "player space" relative to how the pad is held rather than raw axes, accelerate between two thresholds, and never use cut-offs or heavy smoothing ([JoyShockMapper](https://github.com/Electronicks/JoyShockMapper/blob/HEAD/README.md)). Absolute heading drifts without a compass ([GamepadMotionHelpers](https://github.com/JibbSmart/GamepadMotionHelpers)), so pointing uses rotation speed with a clutch button for re-centring, and tilt effects such as steering gravity or flow use only the accelerometer's drift-free gravity direction. The input design treats each pad as **one or two "virtual fingers"**: the left stick moves finger one, the right stick places finger two for pinching and rotating, the analog triggers set pressure or repulsion, shoulder buttons switch tools, and a DualSense or DualShock touchpad maps straight to two fingers. Eight player slots each carry a colour, a cursor shape and a number so colour-blind players can tell them apart, mirrored on DualSense light bars and player lights where WebHID allows, with full remapping, hold-or-toggle options, one-handed presets and support for the Xbox Adaptive Controller, which has no rumble motors ([Chromium](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/device/gamepad/gamepad_device_linux.cc)). Haptics should be short personal accents of 30–120 ms, felt only by the player whose action caused them, renewed in 200–500 ms chunks for sustained effects, timed from the audio clock and always paired with a visual or audio equivalent, in line with Apple's advice to avoid overusing haptics ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/playing-haptics)).

## Music should bend the light that hands are making

System audio, meaning whatever the computer is playing, is the input users expect from MilkDrop and Wallpaper Engine, and Electron 45 covers Windows and macOS with one code path. The page calls `getDisplayMedia({video: true, audio: true})`, which fails without video ([MDN](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mdn/content/main/files/en-us/web/api/mediadevices/getdisplaymedia/index.md)); the main process answers with **`audio: 'loopback'`, documented as supported on Windows and on macOS 14.2 and later** ([Electron](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/electron/electron/main/docs/api/session.md)), never the variant that mutes the speakers; and the page drops the video track. Electron 45 now routes these requests to the permission handler as `display-capture` rather than `media` ([Electron](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/electron/electron/main/docs/breaking-changes.md)). On macOS, capture needs an `NSAudioCaptureUsageDescription` entry and the "System Audio Recording" permission, and **a missing or denied permission fails silently: the track arrives already ended and never delivers sound** ([Electron](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/electron/electron/main/docs/api/desktop-capturer.md)), so an ended track or a silent first second must trigger setup help; Apple's capture API starts at macOS 14.2 ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/coreaudio/capturing-system-audio-with-core-audio-taps)), leaving 13.0–14.1 as best effort. On Linux the same request reaches Chromium's PulseAudio capture of the default output ([Chromium](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/chromium/chromium/main/media/audio/pulse/pulse_loopback_manager.cc)), but Electron promises only Windows and macOS, so PipeWire desktops need testing and a native capture helper in reserve. Microphones and line-in come through `getUserMedia` with echo cancellation, noise suppression and gain control off, and because Electron builds include the patent-encumbered codecs, MP3, AAC, FLAC, Opus and WAV files all play ([Electron](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/electron/electron/main/build/args/all.gn)), which matters because files can be analysed ahead of playback.

Analysis belongs on the CPU in an AudioWorklet running at a fixed step, not on the GPU and not in the render loop. A 2048-point FFT every 256–512 samples (5.3–10.7 ms at 48 kHz) feeds 24–32 bands spaced evenly in pitch, each rising fast, falling slowly and normalised to its own long-term average, the way projectM's bass, mid and treble values hover around 1.0, below 0.7 for quiet passages and above 1.3 for loud ones ([projectM](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/projectM-visualizer/projectm/HEAD/src/libprojectM/Audio/Loudness.hpp)). Changes in each band group give kick, snare and hi-hat hits; comparing the hit pattern with itself over 6–8 seconds gives the tempo; a real-time tracker predicts the next beat as BTrack does ([BTrack](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/adamstark/BTrack/HEAD/README.md)); and a tap or MIDI clock marks the first beat of each bar. All of this costs tens of microseconds per step, whereas a GPU FFT would add milliseconds of overhead for nothing, so the GPU only stores spectrum history for visuals. Driving visuals from the browser's `AnalyserNode` inside the render loop is a trap, because the spec blends each smoothing step with the previous call's output ([Web Audio spec](https://github.com/WebAudio/web-audio-api/blob/HEAD/index.bs)), so its smoothing probably changes with refresh rate; every smoother should use time constants in seconds so 60 Hz and 480 Hz displays look the same. The best existing libraries are copyleft, with Essentia.js AGPL-3.0, aubio GPL-3.0 or later, BTrack GPLv3 and madmom's trained models non-commercial ([Essentia.js](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MTG/essentia.js/HEAD/LICENSE); [aubio](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/aubio/aubio/HEAD/COPYING); [madmom](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/CPJKU/madmom/HEAD/LICENSE)), so the few hundred lines of core signal processing should be written in house, borrowing only permissive code.

Live features arrive **about 30–60 ms after the sound**: 10–21 ms of capture buffering, a 2.7 ms audio block, about 21 ms to the middle of the 2048-point window and up to 17 ms to present a frame. For live input the engine fires beat pulses at the predicted next beat minus that delay and runs a shorter 512-point path for sharp hits; for files it analyses ahead and fires each event when its sound reaches the speakers, using `getOutputTimestamp()` ([MDN](https://github.com/mdn/content/blob/main/files/en-us/web/api/audiocontext/getoutputtimestamp/index.md)). An audio-visual offset slider from −150 to +150 ms with tap-along calibration covers Bluetooth headphones whose delay the app cannot see. Musicians get exact sync from MIDI clock at 24 ticks per quarter note ([WebMidi.js](https://github.com/djipco/webmidi/blob/HEAD/src/Enumerations.js)), which in Electron needs a secure page and an explicit grant ([MDN](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mdn/content/main/files/en-us/web/api/navigator/requestmidiaccess/index.md)), and from OSC received on a main-process UDP socket bound to the local machine by default; Ableton Link is GPL or proprietary ([Ableton](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Ableton/link/HEAD/LICENSE.md)) and can wait.

The mapping rule is what makes Tactilume an instrument rather than a visualiser: **audio scales and offsets the settings players already control, within about ±30%, and its depth drops by 50–80% while someone is steering**, so hands always feel in charge. One "Music reactivity" control (off, subtle, normal, wild) plus per-band sensitivity sets the depth, with slow bass driving large, slow effects, hits driving bursts at the players' touch points, treble driving fine shimmer but never full-screen brightness, the beat timing automatic events such as colour changes on bars, and the musical key driving slow palette drift. Safety arithmetic belongs here too: sixteenth-note hi-hats at 128 BPM arrive about 8.5 times a second, so flashing the whole screen on every hit breaks the three-flashes-per-second limit, while one pulse per beat stays within it up to 180 BPM.

## Ten million particles at 240 Hz needs fields, not neighbours

Particle simulation at this scale is a memory-bandwidth problem. The best measured WebGPU result the research found is **10 million particles simulated and drawn at about 63 fps on a 2016 GTX 1060** ([TU Wien](https://www.cg.tuwien.ac.at/research/publications/2023/PETER-2023-PSW/), search summary only), about 1.6 ns per particle per frame, so 10 million at 120–240 Hz on current cards is realistic once particles use fewer bytes; no WebGPU benchmark at 100 million particles exists anywhere. Methods in which particles read their neighbours reach far less: WebGPU-Ocean's SPH mode managed about 30,000 particles on integrated graphics and its grid-based MLS-MPM mode about 100,000 there and 300,000 on decent GPUs ([WebGPU-Ocean](https://github.com/matsuoka-601/webgpu-ocean/blob/HEAD/README.md)), Splash's experimental branch reached 1.6 million ([Splash](https://github.com/matsuoka-601/Splash/blob/HEAD/README.md)), and a CUDA Barnes–Hut gravity code takes about 150 ms per step at 2 million ([nbody](https://github.com/lechebs/nbody)). That evidence supports three tiers in one engine.

| Tier | Particles | Behaviours | Cost per particle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ambient | 10M–100M tracers | ABC flow, curl noise baked into a 128³ half-float 3D texture (~16 MB), the touch-force grid, a fluid velocity grid, shape and strange attractors | Fixed: a few texture samples or arithmetic steps |
| Interactive | 0.5M–4M, binned into cells | Particle Life species rules, boids, SPH-style separation | Neighbours in 9 (2D) or 27 (3D) cells |
| Liquid | 0.1M–1.6M | MLS-MPM with fixed-point atomic adds into a grid | About 9 (2D) or 27 (3D) atomics |

For the 100-million tier, state should shrink to about 16 bytes per particle, with position as three 21-bit fixed-point coordinates packed into two 32-bit words, half-precision velocity, 16 bits of age and species, and colour derived at render time, stored as separate arrays split by how often they are read so each stays under the binding limit. Many inputs must never mean many loops: ten players with ten touches and 32-segment trails produce 3,200 segments, which tested against 100 million particles would cost 3.2 × 10¹¹ tests per step, so **every input is drawn once per frame into a low-resolution half-float force and velocity texture**, which core WebGPU can blend ([WebGPU spec](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/blob/main/spec/index.bs)), optionally run through a stable-fluids step in the GPU Gems manner ([NVIDIA GPU Gems](https://developer.nvidia.com/gpugems/gpugems/part-vi-beyond-triangles/chapter-38-fast-fluid-dynamics-simulation-gpu)), and sampled once by each particle. Only the handful of gamepad cursors and fixed attractors, up to about 64 point sources, are tested exactly against every particle so they feel crisp, and each player's strength is normalised so one ten-finger phone cannot dominate. These techniques are also the case for custom WGSL rather than a general engine, since fixed-point atomic splats, force-grid input and per-particle choice of drawing method are not what general engines optimise for, with three.js and Babylon.js remaining permissive references to learn from.

Stability and portability need deliberate code. The simulation runs at a fixed 1/240 s step with at most about eight steps per frame, discarding excess time rather than spiralling. WGSL lets compilers assume NaN and infinity never occur, so an `x != x` check may be optimised away ([WGSL](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/blob/main/wgsl/index.bs)), and the guard instead tests the bit pattern through a `bitcast` and respawns bad particles. Sorting and scanning must avoid the fast single-pass "decoupled look-back" technique without a fallback, which **can lock up an entire Apple machine because Apple and ARM GPUs do not guarantee that waiting threads make progress** ([Gridwise](https://github.com/gridwise-webgpu/gridwise/blob/main/docs/scan-and-reduce.md)); PlayCanvas enables its fastest sort only on NVIDIA for the same reason ([PlayCanvas](https://github.com/playcanvas/engine/blob/HEAD/src/scene/graphics/radix-sort/)), so the portable path is multi-pass scanning with counting-sort binning that does not depend on subgroups. Most useful references are permissive, including EA's PB-MPM sample under BSD-3-Clause ([EA](https://github.com/electronicarts/pbmpm)), but one popular GPU sorting repository bundles LGPL-2.1 code under its MIT label, so each file needs checking before it is copied in ([GPUSorting](https://github.com/b0nes164/GPUSorting/blob/HEAD/LICENSE)).

## Fixed-point splats and blurs stand in for ray tracing

WebGPU points are exactly one pixel with no size control ([WebGPU spec](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/blob/main/spec/index.bs)), so the renderer chooses a drawing method per particle by its size on screen. Particles about two pixels across or smaller are splatted by a compute shader that adds their light into fixed-point red, green and blue buffers with 32-bit integer `atomicAdd`, after which a resolve pass converts the sums into an image. Markus Schütz's native compute rasterizer is **10–100 times faster than OpenGL's built-in points and draws about a billion opaque points in about 8 ms on an RTX 3090** ([compute_rasterizer](https://github.com/m-schuetz/compute_rasterizer/blob/master/README.md)); Aras Pranckevičius reports about a fivefold gain over WebGPU's built-in points across more than 30 GPUs, known only from a search summary of the densest test ([Aras](https://aras-p.info/blog/2025/08/24/This-many-points-is-surely-out-of-scope/)); and WebGPU atomics aimed at a single address still reach 62 billion operations a second on an RTX 4090 and 20 billion on an M1 Max ([PWhiddy](https://github.com/PWhiddy/webgpu-atomics-benchmark/blob/main/README.md)). Fixed point is also more correct, because **half-float blending drops faint light: 10,000 additions of 0.001 stall at exactly 4.0 instead of reaching 10**, while integer sums are exact and order-independent, which suggests the current WebGL2 edition already loses brightness in dense regions. Large, soft or blurred sprites stay instanced quads that write into the same fixed-point buffer from their fragment shaders ([WGSL](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/blob/main/wgsl/index.bs)), and because additive light ignores drawing order nothing needs sorting. At 100 million particles, three atomics each become 300 million per frame, too slow for 240 Hz, so the renderer should accumulate per screen tile in shared workgroup memory first (a 32×32 tile of three channels needs 12 KB, well inside the 32 KiB that Direct3D 12 and Apple-silicon Metal expose), keep particles that are near in space near in memory, and combine adds across subgroups where hardware allows.

The "ray-traced" look for this kind of art does not need ray tracing, because **light sources that only add light and never block each other turn global illumination into blurs and second splats**. Pools of light on a virtual floor become a blur whose width follows each particle's height; mirror and water reflections become a second splat of the particles flipped across the floor at about a quarter more splat cost; and volumetric glow and light shafts come from binning particles into a coarse 3D grid of light and density and marching rays through it, the approach Assassin's Creed IV used in about 1.1 ms on 2013 consoles ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/programming/atmospheric-scattering-and-volumetric-fog-algorithm-part-1), search summary only). Only soft shadows around solid shapes need a real lighting method, 2D radiance cascades, whose holographic variant took 1.85 ms at 512×512 on an RTX 3080 laptop GPU ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.02041)) and should run at reduced resolution, while full 3D global illumination costs too much for 240 Hz. The notes' untimed estimate for a mid-range card at 4K and 240 Hz, a 4.17 ms frame, shows how the pieces fit.

| Work per 4K frame at 240 Hz (mid-range GPU, estimated) | Time |
|---|---|
| Simulate and splat about 15M particles | 1.5–2.5 ms |
| Resolve the splat buffers and draw trails | ~0.3 ms |
| Bloom glow | 0.3–0.5 ms |
| Floor light and reflection at half resolution | 0.3–0.6 ms |
| Tone mapping, dithering and the flash guard | 0.2–0.3 ms |
| Browser and compositor overhead | ~0.5 ms |

Temporal anti-aliasing suits additive particles badly, so edges are smoothed at splat time, and trails need half-float buffers with dithering, because at 240 Hz an 8-bit fade freezes almost immediately and an 11-bit one stalls beyond about half a second. Colour is processed in linear light with 1.0 as SDR paper white, then bloom, exposure and one display transform set by the calibrated peak brightness and paper white, then dithering and encoding. ACES 2.0, previewed in OpenColorIO 2.4, takes peak brightness and gamut limits as parameters ([OCIO](https://github.com/AcademySoftwareFoundation/OpenColorIO/blob/main/docs/releases/ocio_2_4.rst)); Polyphony Digital's Gran Turismo 7 operator is HDR-first and MIT-licensed ([CustomToneMappingURP notices](https://github.com/aki-null/CustomToneMappingURP/blob/main/THIRD_PARTY_NOTICES.md)); and Khronos PBR Neutral preserves hue but outputs SDR only ([Khronos](https://github.com/KhronosGroup/ToneMapping/blob/main/PBR_Neutral/README.md)). Capture, the feature Uzu users asked for most, comes almost free when the renderer draws into its own textures and treats the canvas as one destination among several: copies out to memory need rows padded to 256 bytes ([WebGPU spec](https://github.com/gpuweb/gpuweb/blob/main/spec/sections/copies.bs)), and a 4K half-float frame is 66.4 MB, so real-time recording should compress frames on the GPU while high-quality clips render offline at a fixed timestep.

### The flash guard is the one hard requirement

Protecting photosensitive players is the one hard requirement the research produced, and HDR makes it harder. WCAG 2.3.1 allows **no more than three general or red flashes in any second unless the flashing area stays within 0.006 steradians, a quarter of any 10° field of view**, where a general flash is a pair of opposite changes of at least 10% relative luminance with the darker state below 0.80, and a red flash involves saturated red, with red at least 80% of the colour and a chromaticity shift above 0.2 ([WCAG](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/guidelines/terms/20/general-flash-and-red-flash-thresholds.html)). For HDR, WCAG adopts ITU-R BT.1702's absolute rule of a 20 cd/m² change where the darker state is below 160 cd/m², and a Michelson contrast of 1/17 above it ([WCAG Understanding](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/understanding/20/three-flashes-or-below-threshold.html)), so the limiter must work in nits, and a scene can pass in SDR and fail in HDR. Area must be measured as a visual angle: at a 60 cm desk distance, 0.006 sr is about 22 cm², **roughly 1% of a 27-inch screen and about twenty times stricter than the Xbox guideline's 20% of the screen** ([Xbox Accessibility Guideline 118](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/xbox/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/118)), while at 2.5 m from a 65-inch TV it is about 3%, so the app needs screen size and viewing distance, with a cautious desk default. The guard measures the final displayed frame on a coarse grid, counts transitions over real time rather than frames, enforces at 2 flashes per second, the warning level of EA's open-source IRIS checker, by fading offending regions through a gain map, and desaturates red where red transitions accumulate, at an estimated cost under 0.05 ms. IRIS (BSD-3-Clause) runs on recorded video in automated tests ([EA IRIS](https://github.com/electronicarts/IRIS/blob/main/README.md)), and release trailers and sessions need the commercial Harding test, because the free PEAT tool's terms exclude commercial games ([MDN](https://github.com/mdn/content/blob/main/files/en-us/web/accessibility/guides/seizure_disorders/index.md)). No research on HDR-specific photosensitivity thresholds turned up, which argues for capping peak brightness for large-area changes.

## Design a many-handed instrument, not a bigger Uzu

Put together, the research points to an instrument for a room: **many hands, each belonging to a player with their own colour family, tool and sound**, acting on curated "worlds" instead of a ladder of modes. Uzu's finger-count chords could not survive the move anyway, since a phone held in one hand offers one to three fingers and a gamepad two sticks, so the organising principle becomes who is playing and which world they are in. Worlds come from the three simulation tiers (an ambient field, a Particle Life ecology, a liquid, an attractor sculpture), each with its own settings, and they are the unit of curation, sharing and seasonal updates, in the spirit of Panoramical's curated landscapes and Electroplankton's many small toys with one idea each. A handful of verbs, push, pull, swirl, emit, freeze and paint, map onto every controller: phone fingers become strokes in the force grid, phone tilt steers gravity or flow in the HTTPS mode and the app, gamepad sticks drive attractor cursors whose strength the triggers set, the gyro becomes a clutched pointer, and rumble marks collisions and beats. The phone's own screen solves Uzu's discoverability problem without putting controls on the big screen, showing each player's tool, palette and hints and offering a one-tap "surprise me" that answers Uzu users' request for "randomize all".

The "next generation" promise rests on four things no surveyed toy advertises together: HDR highlights that read as real light above SDR white, set by an in-game calibration and capped for large changes; 120–240 Hz motion with smooth trails; several players at once on phones and gamepads; and two-way audio that reacts to whatever is playing and, as a later step, gives each player a generated sound. Comfort is a feature for the audiences that adopted Uzu informally, children, therapy users and autistic users, so the defaults are a calm profile, "reduce flashing" on, a photosensitivity notice before the first music-reactive session, no strobe presets, session timers, a caregiver lock and colour-blind-safe palettes. Capture and sharing ship in version 1 as short looping clips, high-resolution and HDR stills, and scene codes that reproduce a world. Ambient drift, attract, wallpaper and second-screen modes serve the passive use that Reflection and Wallpaper Engine proved, and an optional VJ mode with clean output, MIDI clock and OSC opens club use, though NDI, Spout and Syphon output were not researched and need their own spike. A visible update rhythm answers Uzu's multi-year gaps, and Electron's eight-week majors enforce one anyway. Underneath, one design carries everything: every phone and every gamepad becomes a session that sends timestamped virtual fingers into one input queue, and one simulation event stream drives visuals, audio cues and haptics, so a burst a player causes lights up, sounds and rumbles for that player alone.

| Layer | Choice | Fallback | Biggest open risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shell | Electron 45 (Chromium 156), electron-builder, `app://` protocol, phone server in a utility process | None | Upgrading a major every 8–16 weeks |
| GPU | WebGPU compute, splats or quads chosen by footprint, requested limits | Existing WebGL2 renderer (2M particles) | Budgets unmeasured; forced WebGPU on Linux AMD untested |
| HDR | Half-float canvas with extended tone mapping, sRGB-encoded, user calibration | SDR default look | HDR output not yet seen on any OS |
| Browser phones | WebSocket plus unordered data channel; HTTPS mode with motion | Plain-HTTP touch-only mode | Real-device latency; self-signed click-through behaviour |
| Android app | Kotlin TLS with pinned key plus UDP; system device picker | QR code or typed IP, TCP only | Picker grant scope; bridge latency |
| Gamepads | Gamepad API plus WebHID for Sony and Nintendo pads | Steam Input's virtual pad | Steam Input with WebHID; exclusive device access |
| Audio | Loopback via `getDisplayMedia`; in-house AudioWorklet analysis; MIDI clock and OSC | Microphone, line-in, files | Linux capture under PipeWire; measured latency |
| Safety | Final-frame flash guard in nits; IRIS in tests; Harding for release | Calm profile | HDR thresholds unresearched |
| Distribution | GitHub Releases with electron-updater; Flathub; signed and notarized builds | Store MSIX for free Windows signing | Artifact Signing eligibility; Steam overlay |

## Conclusion

The research shifts where Tactilume's ambition belongs. Particle count, the obvious "next-gen" metric, is the least certain number in the plan: modelled from unsourced factors, capped by bandwidth on the median 8 GB, 1080p Steam PC, and swinging two- to fourfold with data layout. The things that make the product distinctive are cheap and well understood by comparison: drawing every player's input into one force grid, absorbing network jitter, giving each player an identity and their own haptics, and a limiter that lets HDR ship safely. **The competitive edge is many hands, not many particles**, and an engine that fills a 240 Hz frame with six million particles for eight responsive players beats one that pushes fifty million for a single mouse. The same logic settles the requirements question, because requirements stop being a guess once the machine measures itself, the developer's measured profile seeds every similar machine, and opt-in calibration results become the fleet data the research could not find.

The first milestone should therefore be a measurement milestone rather than a feature milestone. Nearly every open risk, from real-GPU particle and splat throughput, HDR output and Wi-Fi phone latency to Linux audio capture, forced WebGPU on AMD, Android 17's picker in practice and Steam Input's effect on WebHID, can be settled within weeks on three computers (a Windows PC with a discrete GPU, an Apple-silicon Mac and a Linux AMD machine) and two phones (an iPhone and the user's Moto G Stylus). Until those numbers exist, every tier, default and protocol choice above is a hypothesis to test, not a fact to build on.
